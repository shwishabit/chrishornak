The Friction Advantage: How to Fulfill Your Resolutions

By Svetlana Whitener

Every January, many of us choose resolutions to improve ourselves. We promise to lose weight, read more or finally get organized. Most of these sincere promises fail, yet we make the same ones year after year. One reason, I believe, is that we try to fulfill them alone. Another is that the ones that matter often require a difficult conversation.

A resolution made alone has no witness. No one reminds you in February or asks how it is going, so it slips away unnoticed. Tell one person what you resolved, a colleague, a friend or a coach, and ask them to check in. A promise someone else has heard is harder to quietly drop.

Busy leaders are adept at putting off difficult conversations, sometimes for years. It may be a direct report who is not working out, or a business partner who needs to think more strategically. These conversations make stomachs tighten, heart rates rise and palms moisten. No wonder they slip off the to-do list.

Emotional intelligence, the field in which I work, offers a way out, and can even turn the problem to advantage. It is the ability to notice feelings, our own and other people’s, to name them, and to use what they tell us to decide what to do.

Antonio Damasio, a neuroscientist at the University of Southern California, argues in his new book, “Natural Intelligence & the Logic of Consciousness,” that without feelings, true intelligence cannot exist. Alongside the parts of the mind that perceive and that think in words, he describes a long-underappreciated feeling mind. It monitors heartbeat, breathing and blood chemistry, and works to “continuously qualify” everything else as positive, neutral or negative.

David Eagleman, a Stanford neuroscientist, calls the boldest version of this “harder to establish.” But the narrower point, that feelings tell us what matters, seems to me remarkably useful, though it defies what many of us were taught: leave your feelings outside the office.

How, then, do we use our feelings? First, by paying them directed attention. A tight stomach and racing heart before a hard conversation are the kind of signals Damasio’s feeling mind reads: the body marking something as high stakes. The dread is not noise to be shrugged off or waited out. It is information about what we value. A comfortable resolution never sends that signal, so nothing in us has a reason to change. In other words, the friction may be the advantage.

This does not mean every uncomfortable feeling calls for lengthy soul searching. Some dread is only a light warning that we are about to say something unfair, or say it in anger. To tell which is which, name the feeling, then ask what it is protecting. If the answer is my pride, or my wish to win, slow down and choose your words. If it is a relationship, a standard or a promise, it is a signal to act. I think of the result as compassionate accountability: saying the hard thing, with care.

So, before you firmly resolve to resolve whatever you’re going to resolve, ask yourself one question: Is there a hard conversation I’ve been avoiding? If the answer is yes, that conversation belongs at the top of your list. A comfortable resolution is “delegate more.” An uncomfortable one is telling your operations director on Monday that the budget is now hers.

A single conversation is easy to postpone, so I ask clients to turn it into weekly and monthly goals. Each week: Did I have the conversation, or find a reason not to? Each month: What has changed because I did? The AI reflection tool I built for my practice can prompt those check-ins. It cannot feel the tight stomach for anyone, and if Damasio is right, that feeling is the part that moves us.

Here in Marin, January brings a hike up Mount Tamalpais and a new meditation app. Nothing wrong with those. But if you lead people, put the difficult conversations first. The better time you keep waiting for is probably never going to come.

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and founder of InLight Coaching.
