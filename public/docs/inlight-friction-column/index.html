The Friction Advantage: Why Comfortable Resolutions Fail

By Svetlana Whitener

Every January, many of us choose resolutions that are designed not to hurt. We promise to get up earlier, read more or finally get organized. That, I think, is precisely why so many of them fail. Not one of them asks us to have a difficult conversation with another human being.

For leaders, the resolution that would change the year is often a postponed conversation. It may be with a direct report who is not working out, or with a business partner who keeps making decisions alone. These conversations make the stomach tighten, and so they quietly slip off the list.

Emotional intelligence, the field I work in, suggests a different reading of that tight stomach. It is not simply anxiety to be managed. It may be the most useful information we have.

I recently read a review of a new book by Antonio Damasio, a neuroscientist at the University of Southern California who has spent three decades arguing that reason rests on feeling. In “Natural Intelligence & the Logic of Consciousness,” he argues that without feelings, true intelligence cannot exist. His first book, “Descartes’ Error,” described patients who lost access to signals from their own bodies and, with them, much of their ability to make decisions.

Not everyone accepts the boldest version of his claim. David Eagleman, a Stanford neuroscientist who reviewed the book, calls it “harder to establish.” But the narrower point seems to me remarkably useful. Our feelings tell us what matters. Yet many of us were taught, essentially, to leave them at the office door.

The review describes the mind as a film reel and the feeling body as its projector. Anesthesia switches off the projector, and the film keeps running in the dark with no one watching. A comfortable resolution may do something similar to a year. The plan keeps running. But because nothing in it asks us to feel anything, very little in us changes.

In other words, the friction may be the advantage. The discomfort before a hard conversation is often a signal that something we value is at stake. A resolution that never produces that feeling is probably not aimed at anything that matters.

This does not mean every uncomfortable feeling should be obeyed. Some dread is a warning. It can mean we are about to say something unfair, or to say it in anger. Emotional intelligence is the ability to tell the difference, and to ask what the feeling is pointing at. I think of the result as compassionate accountability: saying the hard thing, with care.

So when someone tells me in January what this year’s resolution will be, I ask a simple question: Whom will you have to talk to? A comfortable resolution is “delegate more.” An uncomfortable one is telling your operations director on Monday that the budget is now hers. A comfortable resolution is “communicate better.” An uncomfortable one is asking your co-founder why the two of you keep having the same argument.

A single conversation is remarkably easy to postpone, so I ask clients to turn it into weekly and monthly goals. Each week: Did I have the conversation, or did I find a reason not to? Each month: What has changed because I did? The AI reflection tool I built for my practice can prompt those check-ins. It cannot feel the tight stomach for anyone, and if Damasio is right, that feeling is precisely the part that moves us.

Here in Marin, many of us are quite good at self-improvement. January brings the earlier hike up Mount Tamalpais and the new meditation app. There is nothing wrong with either. But for those of us who lead other people, they are rarely what the year is asking for.

So I want to encourage you to put the hardest conversation at the top of the list. The better time we keep waiting for may never come. Which conversation have you been saving for it?

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and founder of InLight Coaching.
