The Friction Advantage: Why Comfortable Resolutions Fail

By Svetlana Whitener

Every January, many of us choose resolutions to improve ourselves. We promise to lose weight, read more or finally get organized. Most of these sincere promises fail, and we make the same ones year after year. The reason, I believe, is that we undertake them alone, without a sometimes difficult conversation with another human being.

Busy leaders, always finding something more pressing to do, sometimes put off difficult conversations for years. It may be with a direct report who is not working out, or with a business partner who needs to be more strategic in her thinking. These conversations make stomachs tighten, heart rates increase and palms moisten. It is easy to see why they so often quietly slip off the to-do list.

Emotional intelligence, the field in which I work, suggests a way to turn this problem to advantage.

Antonio Damasio, a neuroscientist at the University of Southern California, has spent three decades studying the matter. In his new book, “Natural Intelligence & the Logic of Consciousness,” he argues that without feelings, true intelligence cannot exist. He describes the mind as three interwoven parts: one that perceives the world around us, one that thinks in words and plans, and a third, long underappreciated, the feeling mind. It monitors breathing, heartbeat, temperature and blood chemistry, and it works to “continuously qualify” everything else as positive, neutral or negative. A mind, in his account, emerges only when a living body gives what happens inside it urgency, value and stakes.

Not everyone accepts the boldest version of this claim. David Eagleman, a Stanford neuroscientist, calls it “harder to establish.” But the narrower point, that our feelings tell us what matters, seems to me remarkably useful, even though it flies in the face of what many of us were taught: when you arrive at the office, leave your feelings outside.

So how do we look to our feelings? The tight stomach and racing heart before a hard conversation are the kind of signals Damasio’s feeling mind reads. They are the body marking something as high stakes. The dread is not noise to push through or wait out. It is information about what we value. Looking to it means pausing to ask what is at stake: a relationship, a standard, a promise. A comfortable resolution never sends that signal, so nothing in us has a reason to change. In other words, the friction may be the advantage.

This does not mean every uncomfortable feeling should be obeyed. Some dread is a warning that we are about to say something unfair, or say it in anger. Emotional intelligence is the ability to tell the difference, and to ask what the feeling is pointing at. I think of the result as compassionate accountability: saying the hard thing, with care.

So before you firmly resolve to resolve whatever you’re going to resolve, ask yourself a simple question: Is there a hard conversation I’ve been avoiding? If the answer is yes, that is the very thing that should go at the top of your list. A comfortable resolution is “delegate more.” An uncomfortable one is telling your operations director on Monday that the budget is now hers.

A single conversation is remarkably easy to postpone, so I ask clients to turn it into weekly and monthly goals. Each week: Did I have the conversation, or did I find a reason not to? Each month: What has changed because I did? The AI reflection tool I built for my practice can prompt those check-ins. It cannot feel the tight stomach for anyone, and if Damasio is right, that feeling is precisely the part that moves us.

Here in Marin, many of us are quite good at self-improvement. January brings an early hike up Mount Tamalpais and a new meditation app, and there’s certainly nothing wrong with those. But for those of us who lead people, they are rarely what most needs our attention.

So put those difficult conversations first. The better time you keep waiting for is probably never going to come.

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and founder of InLight Coaching.
