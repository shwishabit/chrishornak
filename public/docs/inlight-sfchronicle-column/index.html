The gold is not only in the session. It is in everything after you leave.

By Svetlana Whitener

Coaching can generate a long series of excellent conversations and remarkably little change. The session is where a decision is made. The days afterward are where it is kept or quietly abandoned. My profession has built almost everything around the hour. We schedule it, prepare for it, and train coaches to run it well. Far less attention goes to the Thursday afternoon three weeks later, where the change is actually won or lost.

Most of the people I coach leave a session knowing exactly what they mean to do. Far fewer are still holding it by the following Wednesday. Nothing has gone wrong, exactly. The week simply happened to them, the way it happens to everyone. That gap has taught me more about this work than anything that takes place in the room.

The gold is not only in the coaching session itself. It is in everything that happens after you leave.

What my clients lack is almost never understanding. Senior people can usually describe the change they need with real precision, and describing it is not the difficult part. Doing it, while someone waits on the other side of a desk and the exchange is going badly, is the difficult part.

An hour is a small thing to weigh against an ordinary week. What has been learned so well during a session may simply disappear—not because anybody stopped caring, but because Tuesday shows up with plans of its own.

The things that undo it are rarely dramatic. A meeting that overruns. An email you must answer before you can think. The colleague who behaves exactly as they always behave, in the very moment you had promised to answer differently. And underneath all of it runs the silent conversation you have been having with yourself for years, which does not pause politely for a coaching engagement.

By the next appointment, the intention is often gone.

So I started asking a different question. Not how to improve the hour, which I already know how to do, but what might carry the time between one hour and the next. I built an artificial intelligence companion for my own practice and named it the InLight Companion.

It pays attention to the word should. When a person keeps telling it what they ought to do rather than what they want, it says as much, because the distance between those two words is usually where the trouble sits. For years I have asked clients to carry one question through an ordinary day: do I benefit from having this thought? Almost nobody holds it alone for two weeks.

Amy, a chief operating officer, left a session resolved to stop rescuing her team. By Thursday she had rewritten a proposal herself, under pressure, telling herself it was faster. That evening the Companion noted how often she had said should, and asked whether rescuing them served the boundary she set on Tuesday. The next morning she spoke to the person instead of taking over.

I did not build it to be agreeable. Software is extremely good at agreeing with people, and agreement rarely changes anyone. Being kind is harder than being nice. It calls for sincerity, it usually calls for action, and it can be uncomfortable for both parties. Nice tells you the plan sounds excellent. Kind asks why it is the same plan you described in March. Nice keeps the exchange pleasant, and pleasant is the enemy of most difficult decisions.

A tool that cannot make you uncomfortable may not be much use to you.

It also has to recognize its own edge. When a matter belongs with a person rather than a program, it says so and hands the conversation back. That limit was deliberate, and it is the first thing I would want anyone buying one of these tools to ask about.

You are entitled to know where I stand. I built this thing, my practice uses it, and I now offer it to clients. For years I have argued in print that a machine cannot do what a coach does, and I have not changed my mind about that. What I have changed my mind about is the idea that the hour is the whole of the work.

Which is why this is not really an argument about software. It is an argument about where change actually occurs, and much less of it occurs in the room where you decide to change than we like to think. Anyone who has walked out of a good meeting with a clear plan, and been unable a week later to say what became of it, already understands the point. The question is not whether a machine can coach you. It cannot.

What is holding your best thinking between the day you have it and the day you need it?

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and the founder of InLight Coaching. She is the creator of the InLight Companion, an artificial intelligence reflection tool she built for her own coaching practice and now offers to clients.

Why this is not the Marin piece again

The Marin column argued that artificial intelligence should widen real conversation rather than price it out of reach, and that a tool has to hand you back to a person. That argument is spent. This one makes a different claim: the hour is not where change happens. The days after it are, and very little in coaching is aimed at them.

It also does not repeat the 2024 Forbes article, which concluded that AI belongs alongside coaching rather than instead of it. This draft says that out loud rather than pretending the earlier piece does not exist, and then goes somewhere it never went.
