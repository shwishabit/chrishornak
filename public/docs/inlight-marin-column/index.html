Marin Voice · Opinion

A real conversation is becoming a luxury good. Marin should notice.

By Svetlana Whitener

InLight Coaching · Novato

There was a man in a story I read this month who'd been messaging a woman on a dating app, and he was using a chatbot to write his side, and she seemed to be doing the same back at him. He gave up and typed the actual question: “How long are we gonna ChatGPT each other?”

She never responded. Which is an answer, I suppose.

The story ran in The New York Times Magazine on August 14, and the reporter, Callie Holtermann, calls this a bot loop. Both people hand their half of the conversation to software, then stand around supervising the result.

Soheil Feizi, an associate professor of computer science at the University of Maryland, says two AI systems built on the same technology don't catch each other's mistakes. “They are just sharing the same blind spots.” They agree, is the problem. They agree very fluently.

I've been coaching executives in Marin for how many years? years, and my first thought reading this had nothing to do with technology. It was about a different loop, one with no software in it anywhere.

Someone sits down across from me, still wearing the jacket from whatever meeting they came out of, and walks me through a conversation that went badly. Partway through they stop and say the thing they should have said. They knew it then, too. They knew it in the parking garage afterward. And a week later I'm hearing about that same conversation going the same way again.

The insight was there and it was real, and it still didn't survive the drive home.

That distance is my whole line of work. It has never been an information problem.

The part that unsettled me most wasn't about errors. Sarah Davis, a cultural anthropologist who is dean of St. John's College in Santa Fe, wonders whether people might get so used to how smooth a machine conversation feels that they lose their tolerance for the other kind, the unpredictable kind, the kind with a person in it. What she names as the risk is friction.

That sounds an awful lot like my job description.

Coaching is friction. Somebody tells me what they've decided and I don't agree and we sit in that a while, and it's uncomfortable, and it is supposed to be uncomfortable. Smooth that over and you haven't built a coaching tool, you've built an anesthetic. Of course people will use it. It feels better.

That's what I had in front of me while I built a tool for my own practice when?. It's called the InLight Companion, and it notices when somebody says “I should” instead of “I want to,” which sounds like a small distinction and isn't one.

It also knows when to stop. When a conversation goes somewhere a reflection tool has no business going, it ends, points the person toward real help, and tells me they need me. That wasn't a feature I bolted on at the end. The rest is built around it.

Holtermann ends somewhere I didn't see coming. If bot loops keep spreading, getting an actual human being on the other end of the line stops being ordinary and turns into a rare privilege, priced accordingly. The people best able to afford it are the ones the AI boom is making rich.

Look around. That's us, or it's our neighbors, or it's whoever's ahead of us in line at which Marin spot?.

So it isn't whether we'll still get to talk to people. We will. We can pay for it. It's whether that becomes a thing we quietly buy for ourselves and let everybody else go without.

I don't have a tidy answer to that one. What I have is a test I run before I let any of these tools near my practice, and it has nothing to do with whether the thing sounds human. They all sound human now. Holtermann calls that output “dazzling in their phoniness, like costume jewelry.”

The test is just this. When it gets something wrong, who finds out?

If the answer is another machine, you've built a loop, and the mistake keeps going around inside it until somebody outside notices. If the answer is a person who knows the human involved and has to answer for what happens to them, you've built a different thing. Same technology underneath. Not the same tool.

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and the founder of InLight Coaching.

Why the statistics are gone

Every figure in the earlier draft traced back to a single web page: careertrainer.ai/en/reports/ai-coaching-statistics. The 23 million users, the $2.4 billion by 2028, the 62% of Fortune 500, the 85% / 80% / 34% performance claims. All of it, one page.

That page belongs to a vendor. There is a row of consultancy logos across the top of it, but not one number on the page is attributed to any of them. No study. No citation. No footnote.

An editor will check at least one of those figures before running an opinion piece, and the check ends where mine did. That is a correction notice with Svetlana's name on it. Worth knowing before the numbers get used anywhere else, LinkedIn included.

The line about being the first coach in Marin County to launch an AI companion came out for the same reason. There is no way to verify it.

The column does not need any of it. The Times piece is a stronger foundation than the statistics were, and every fact in this draft can be checked in about two minutes.
