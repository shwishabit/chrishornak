Marin Voice · Opinion

A Real Conversation Is Often a Luxury. Is It a Necessity, Nevertheless.

By Svetlana Whitener

InLight Coaching · Novato

I read a story in The New York Times this month about a man on a dating app who discovered that the woman he was talking with was doing exactly what he was doing: using artificial intelligence to write her messages.

“How long are we going to continue ChatGPTing each other?” he finally asked her.

She never responded.

Two people who presumably wanted to get to know each other had delegated the getting-to-know-you part to machines. The article described such exchanges as “bot loops”—situations in which machines increasingly communicate with other machines on behalf of human beings. But what struck me was not primarily the technology of the matter. It was what the humans were giving up.

I have spent how many years? years coaching executives and other professionals in Marin County. Again and again, clients come to me after a difficult conversation—with a colleague, an employee, a spouse or a business partner—and tell me what they wish they had said.

Often, they knew it almost immediately. They knew it walking back to their car. They knew it driving home. Yet a week later, they sometimes found themselves having essentially the same conversation and making essentially the same mistake.

The problem is rarely a lack of information. They know what they should do. The difficult part is learning to do it when another human being is sitting across from them.

Sarah Davis, a cultural anthropologist and dean of St. John's College in Santa Fe, has raised an intriguing concern about AI: If we become accustomed to the smoothness of conversations with machines, will we gradually lose our tolerance for conversations with actual people?

Real people interrupt us. They misunderstand us. They disagree. They become impatient or defensive. Sometimes they say precisely the thing we hoped they wouldn't say.

In other words, human conversation contains friction. So does coaching. Someone tells me what he has decided, and I question it. Someone explains why she had no choice, and I ask whether that is really true. There may be a long silence. There may be irritation. Sometimes I tell a client something the client very much does not want to hear.

That discomfort is not a defect in the process. It is often the point of the process.

Remove all the friction and you may not have created a better coaching tool. You may simply have created an anesthetic. Of course people will choose it. It feels better.

When the machine gets something wrong, who finds out?

I had to confront this issue myself when I developed an AI-based reflection tool for my coaching practice, called the InLight Companion. The question was never whether a machine could be made to sound like me. Technology has become remarkably good at that.

The harder question was: What should the machine not be allowed to do?

My answer was that it should never pretend to replace the human relationship at the center of coaching. It can help someone notice patterns. It can recognize, for example, when a person repeatedly says “I should” rather than “I want to”—a small linguistic difference that can reveal a much larger conflict.

But it also has to know when to stop. If a conversation enters territory where an automated reflection tool does not belong, the tool should say so. It should direct the person toward real help. In my practice, it should bring the human coach back into the conversation.

The New York Times article raised a possibility I find disturbing: As automated interactions become ordinary, access to an actual attentive human being may become increasingly uncommon—and increasingly expensive.

Already we see hints of this everywhere. While wanting to talk to a real human, we find ourselves having to navigate through layers of bot voices. Algorithms decide which questions we are permitted to ask and which answers we receive. And now AI is involving itself in some of our most personal exchanges.

In affluent Marin County, it is easy to imagine responding to this trend by simply buying our way around it. We can often hire the therapist, coach, tutor, financial adviser or other professional who will sit across from us, listen carefully and respond to us individually. But what happens when this genuine human attention costs more than we can afford or are willing to pay? And what happens to the people in this county who never had that option at all?

I am not opposed to artificial intelligence. I use it. I have built with it. Used carefully, it can help people think, reflect and prepare themselves for difficult conversations.

But I have developed a simple test for deciding whether technology is strengthening a human relationship or quietly replacing one.

When the machine gets something wrong, who finds out?

If the answer is another machine, we may have created a loop in which errors, assumptions and misunderstandings simply circulate. If the answer is a human being who knows the person involved, notices what has happened and accepts responsibility for what comes next, we have created something different. The technology underneath may be the same. The relationship is not.

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and the founder of InLight Coaching.

Why the statistics are gone

Every figure in the earlier draft traced back to a single web page: careertrainer.ai/en/reports/ai-coaching-statistics. The 23 million users, the $2.4 billion by 2028, the 62% of Fortune 500, the 85% / 80% / 34% performance claims. All of it, one page.

That page belongs to a vendor. There is a row of consultancy logos across the top of it, but not one number on the page is attributed to any of them. No study. No citation. No footnote.

An editor will check at least one of those figures before running an opinion piece, and the check ends where mine did. That is a correction notice with Svetlana's name on it. Worth knowing before the numbers get used anywhere else, LinkedIn included.

The line about being the first coach in Marin County to launch an AI companion came out for the same reason. There is no way to verify it.

The column does not need any of it. The Times piece is a stronger foundation than the statistics were, and every fact in this draft can be checked in about two minutes.
