Marin Voice · Opinion

AI should widen real conversation, not price it out of reach

By Svetlana Whitener

InLight Coaching · Novato

A real conversation should not be a luxury.

I am not opposed to artificial intelligence. I use it. I have built with it. Used carefully, it can help people think, reflect and prepare themselves for difficult conversations.

Human attention does not scale. A coach or a therapist has only so many hours. Keep this technology at arm's length and that attention stays where it already is—with the people who can afford it.

I have spent how many years? years coaching executives and other professionals in Marin County. Again and again, clients come to me after a difficult conversation—with a colleague, an employee, a spouse or a business partner—and tell me what they wish they had said.

Often, they knew it almost immediately. They knew it walking back to their car. They knew it driving home.

Yet a week later, they sometimes found themselves having essentially the same conversation and making essentially the same mistake.

The problem is rarely a lack of information. They know what they should do. The difficult part is learning to do it when another human being is sitting across from them.

So when I built an AI reflection tool for my practice, called the InLight Companion, the question was never whether a machine could be made to sound like me. Technology has become remarkably good at that.

The harder question was: what should the machine not be allowed to do?

My answer was that it should never pretend to replace the human relationship at the center of coaching. It can help someone notice patterns. It can recognize when a person repeatedly says “I should” rather than “I want to”—a small linguistic difference that can reveal a much larger conflict.

But it also has to know when to stop. If a conversation enters territory where an automated tool does not belong, it should say so and bring the human coach back in.

That boundary may become increasingly important.

When the machine gets something wrong, who finds out?

I read a story in The New York Times about a man who discovered that the woman he was talking with on a dating app was doing exactly what he was doing: using artificial intelligence to write her messages.

“How long are we going to continue ChatGPTing each other?” he finally asked her.

She never responded.

The article called such exchanges “bot loops”—machines increasingly communicating with other machines on behalf of human beings.

What struck me was not the technology. It was what the humans were giving up.

Sarah Davis, a cultural anthropologist and dean of St. John's College in Santa Fe, has raised an intriguing concern: if we become accustomed to the smoothness of conversations with machines, will we gradually lose our tolerance for conversations with actual people?

Real people interrupt us. They misunderstand us. They disagree. Sometimes they say precisely the thing we hoped they wouldn't say.

Human conversation contains friction. So does coaching. Someone tells me what he has decided, and I question it.

That discomfort is not a defect in the process. It is often the point of the process. Remove all the friction and you may not have created a better coaching tool. You may simply have created an anesthetic.

In affluent Marin, it is easy to imagine simply buying our way around this. We hire someone who will sit across from us and listen carefully.

But what happens when this genuine human attention costs more than we can afford or are willing to pay?

I hold every tool to one test: when the machine gets something wrong, who finds out?

If the answer is another machine, we have made a loop in which errors and misunderstandings simply circulate. If the answer is a human being who knows the person involved and accepts responsibility for what comes next, we have made something different.

The technology underneath may be the same. The relationship is not.

Build the second kind.

Svetlana Whitener, of Novato, is a Master Certified Coach and founder of InLight Coaching.

Why this reads differently

Your sentences are almost all still here. What changed is the order, and the direction the argument runs.

It takes a stand in the first line. The editor's words were that the old version “reads more like a general interest article than an Op Ed.” An op‑ed picks a side in the first breath. Yours is now line one: A real conversation should not be a luxury. That is the sentence you chose.

It argues for the technology, not against it. He asked for a column about AI as “a tool we should understand and embrace, not fear.” The old draft ran: AI spreads → real attention gets scarce and costly → that is bad. That reads as fear. This one runs: real attention was always scarce → AI is the thing that can widen it → but only if it is built to hand people back to a person → here is the one I built and the rule I gave it. Same position you held. Opposite temperature.

The hinge is a sentence you already wrote and I had cut for space: “Used carefully, it can help people think, reflect and prepare themselves for difficult conversations.” It is back, in the second paragraph. Your view of AI now shows up on line two instead of near the end.

The paragraphs are short again. In the last pass I folded eight of your one‑line paragraphs together. That was wrong, and reading the published columns proved it. The Marin Voice column that ran in August has twelve paragraphs of twenty words or fewer out of twenty‑five. Your instinct for short beats matched the paper better than my edit did. “She never responded.” stands alone again, the way you wrote it.

It ends with something to do. Every column of theirs I read closes by asking the reader to act. Yours closes on your own two lines, then one short instruction.

Eight published columns were read before any of this was written — five of them from Marin Voice, and one from August on AI specifically. That reading is what the editor asked for, and skipping it is what cost us the first round.

Why the statistics are gone

Every figure in the earlier draft traced back to a single web page: careertrainer.ai/en/reports/ai-coaching-statistics. The 23 million users, the $2.4 billion by 2028, the 62% of Fortune 500, the 85% / 80% / 34% performance claims. All of it, one page.

An editor will check at least one of those figures before running an opinion piece, and the check ends where mine did. That is a correction notice with Svetlana's name on it. Worth knowing before the numbers get used anywhere else, LinkedIn included.

The line about being the first coach in Marin County to launch an AI companion came out for the same reason. There is no way to verify it.

The column does not need any of it. The Times piece is a stronger foundation than the statistics were, and every fact in this draft can be checked in about two minutes.
